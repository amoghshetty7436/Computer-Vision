Where does 3D reconstruction from photos break?

We stress-tested COLMAP, the most widely used tool for turning a set of photos into a 3D model, under bad weather, desert conditions, image noise, AI photo edits, AI upscaling and tourist crowds. We ran about 1,400 reconstructions.

The problem

Structure from Motion (SfM) takes ordinary photos of a place, taken from different spots, and works out two things: where each photo was taken from, and a 3D model of the scene. It powers 3D maps, virtual tours, heritage preservation, film effects, AR apps and robot navigation.

COLMAP (Schönberger & Frahm, CVPR 2016) is the standard SfM tool and the paper this project is about. It was shown to work on clean tourist photos in daylight. Real photos are often taken at night, in rain, fog or sandstorms, in crowds, or after an AI app has "enhanced" them. The paper doesn't test any of that, so we did.

The data

We used two building photo sets released with COLMAP, plus real driving footage recorded in many kinds of weather.

How SfM works, and how we score it

  1. Find keypoints. Distinctive spots like window corners and brick edges, about 8,000 per photo, found with an algorithm called SIFT.
  2. Match them between every pair of photos.
  3. Throw out wrong matches with RANSAC, a voting method that keeps only matches that agree on one consistent camera geometry.
  4. Place the cameras one photo at a time and build the 3D points. A photo that gets placed is called registered.

The score: AUC@10

For every pair of photos we compare how the camera moved between the two shots (how much it turned, and in which direction it moved) with the true movement. Each pair gets credit from 0 to 1:

Credit1.00.90.50.100

AUC@10 is the average credit over all pairs. 1.0 is perfect, and clean photos score 0.96. A photo that couldn't be placed scores 0 in every pair it belongs to, so a method can't look good by quietly dropping hard photos. We also report the registered fraction, the share of photos that got placed at all. A high registered fraction with a low AUC is a silent failure: the model looks complete but is wrong.

Every setting was run 3 times (different random seeds) on both buildings, and we report the average of those 6 runs.

Experiment 1: Weather and desert

Question: How badly do rain, snow, fog, night and desert conditions (sandstorm, heat shimmer, harsh sun) hurt SfM? Does simulated weather behave like real weather?
  • Real night is the only real condition that breaks SfM (0.02). Rain (0.87) and snow on the ground (0.88) are fine.
  • Desert: heat shimmer is almost harmless (0.77 even at maximum). Harsh sun, which washes out detail, is the worst desert effect.
  • Fake weather misleads. Simulated fog and snow are much harsher than the real thing, and simulated night is much milder (0.33 vs 0.02 for real night).

Experiment 2: Noise that gets stronger, ordinary vs AI-generated

Question: As damage to the photos increases, where does SfM break? Do AI edits fail differently from ordinary noise and blur?
  • Motion blur and JPEG are the worst ordinary damage. Light motion blur already drops the score to 0.60.
  • The lightest Stable Diffusion edit scores 0.00. 70% of the keypoints survive, but the model redraws brick and leaves differently in every photo, so they stop matching: good matches on one pair fell from 344 to 17.
  • LaMa inpainting is harmless compared with grey holes, because its fills are smooth and SIFT mostly ignores them.
  • The rule we found: the more fine texture an AI invents, the more it hurts 3D, however real the photo looks.

Experiment 3: AI upscaling before SfM

Question: If the photos are low resolution, does an AI super-resolution model help SfM?
  • At 4× upscaling: bicubic 0.31, Real-ESRGAN 0.11, Stable Diffusion upscaler 0.10, raw low-res 0.02.
  • Plain upscaling helps a lot. AI upscaling is worse than plain upscaling, because it invents different detail in each photo.

Experiment 4: Tourist crowds

Question: How many tourists does it take to break SfM, and do the usual fixes work?
  • 40 people per photo: score 0.04, yet 89% of photos still get placed. This is a silent failure.
  • Masking and AI erasing help equally (0.32 each). The cheap fix, a mask, is enough.

Experiment 5: How many bad photos does it take?

Question: If only some photos are bad, do they just drop out, or do they spoil the good ones too?
  • Bad photos spoil good ones. With just 10% of photos motion-blurred, the score falls from 0.92 to 0.60.
  • LaMa-edited photos are tolerated until about half the set.
  • Takeaway: filtering out bad photos before running SfM is cheap and very effective.

Experiment 6: Do modern AI features fix it?

Question: Which part fails: the keypoints, the matcher or the camera-placing step? Would modern learned methods (ALIKED keypoints, the LightGlue matcher, the GLOMAP global solver) do better?
  • On clean photos, classic COLMAP wins easily (0.96 vs 0.35 to 0.56 for the learned pipelines).
  • On damaged photos, learned features win, often by 3 to 4×. LightGlue is the only pipeline that survives motion blur (0.28), and on AI-upscaled photos it scores 0.41 against SIFT's 0.00.
  • The learned matcher gets confused by look-alike facades: it confidently matches one side of the building to another and places whole groups of photos about 90° off. GLOMAP has the same problem.

Experiment 7: Does a sunny-day map work in other seasons?

Question: If you build a map on a sunny day, can you still locate new photos taken in snow, rain or at night? This is what self-driving cars and AR need.
  • Overcast, snow and rain photos are located well: 87% to 97% get placed, with scores of 0.76 to 0.90.
  • Not one of 240 night photos could be located in the daytime map.

What it all means

  1. Night is the condition that really breaks SfM, and simulating it underestimates how bad it is.
  2. AI-edited and AI-upscaled photos can look perfect and still be useless for 3D.
  3. Many failures are silent. Checking how many photos got placed isn't enough.
  4. A few bad photos spoil the rest, so filter before you reconstruct.
  5. No method wins everywhere. Classic COLMAP is best on clean photos, learned methods are best on damaged ones, and learned matching struggles with symmetric buildings.
Glossary
Pose
Where a camera was and which way it pointed.
Keypoint / feature
A distinctive spot in a photo that can be found again in another photo.
SIFT
The classic hand-designed keypoint method (2004) that COLMAP uses.
RANSAC
Repeatedly guesses the geometry from a few random matches and keeps the guess most matches agree with. It's robust to random errors but can be fooled by consistent ones, such as look-alike facades.
Registered / registered fraction
A photo that was placed in the model, and the share of photos placed.
AUC@10
Average credit over all photo pairs: full credit for a perfect pair, falling to zero at 10° of error or when a photo isn't placed.
Incremental vs global SfM
Incremental adds one photo at a time (COLMAP). Global solves all photos at once (GLOMAP).
ALIKED, LightGlue
Neural-network keypoints and a neural-network matcher, used as published and not retrained.
LaMa
A neural network that fills holes in an image.
Real-ESRGAN
A GAN that enlarges images by inventing detail.
Stable Diffusion / SD-Turbo
An AI image generator. Its "img2img" mode redraws an existing photo.
Seed
A random-number setting. We repeat each run with 3 seeds.

Nothing was trained in this project: every model was used as published. Computation ran in a cloud container (CPU) and on the NERSC Perlmutter supercomputer (about 2.8 GPU-hours). Code, raw results and the detailed PDF report: github.com/amoghshetty7436/Computer-Vision.
